Motivation

Collaborative assembly is slowed when a worker must stop, search for a component, or translate an underspecified request such as “bring me the red gear” into the robot’s rigid vocabulary. Gesture-only interfaces can also require the worker to reposition their body and point precisely, while language-only interfaces may fail when several parts share a name. The H2R CoAssembly project addresses this coordination gap by combining speech, gesture, mixed-reality scene information, and the current assembly state. Its central question is: Does task awareness reduce the effort and ambiguity of requesting parts during human–robot assembly compared with gesture alone or language plus gesture?

The project builds directly on prior HRI and AR research. Lambrecht and Nimpsch [1] found that natural, predictive requests can support flexible just-in-time handovers, but may increase cognitive workload; this motivates measuring both successful retrieval and workload. Liu et al. [2] incorporated task context into learning collaborative assembly assistance, motivating our explicit task graph as a transparent and testable context model. Zigart and Schlund [3] compared spatial AR with conventional assembly support and motivate our hands-free Meta Quest 3 interface. Lunding et al. [4] used AR to improve workspace awareness and coordinate parallel assembly, while our work extends the idea toward multimodal, task-state-aware part referral. Together, these studies motivate an integrated system whose assistance is grounded in the worker’s language, visible pointing behavior, and the set of parts that are valid at the current step.

Proposed Methodology

The technical system will represent a four-row educational gearbox as a dependency-based task graph. Each step will be labeled READY, BLOCKED, or COMPLETED; completing a step consumes raw components and creates a named subassembly. A Meta Quest 3 client will visualize relevant components and collect pointing/gesture input. A speech interface and vision-language component will resolve flexible references—including row number, color, shape, side, size, and location—to canonical objects. The state engine will constrain responses to active parts and currently feasible actions, preventing the assistant from recommending consumed parts or blocked steps. Robot-side Python services will communicate with the mixed-reality Unity application, highlight or retrieve the selected part, and log events for replay and analysis.

The empirical component will use a three-condition within-subjects design: (1) Gesture, (2) Language + Gesture, and (3) Task-Aware multimodal assistance. Interface condition is the independent variable. Retrieval accuracy, request time, correction count, workload, confidence, clarity/ease, adaptation burden, and preference are dependent variables. Condition order will use counterbalancing to reduce learning and carryover effects. Participants will perform comparable part-request trials during gearbox assembly, with row identity encoded redundantly by number, color, and shape. Trial logs, questionnaires, and observations provide triangulation across behavioral and self-report evidence. A brief post-condition question will serve as a manipulation check that participants noticed and understood the available interaction cues.

The study will recruit approximately 18–24 adult participants, subject to course time and equipment access. This target improves on the existing pilot-scale N=11 data and is large enough to detect effects in the approximate range already observed for workload and clarity, while remaining feasible for a semester project. The study’s unit of analysis will be the participant for condition-level comparisons. We will define each measure before analysis through explicit operationalization: for example, retrieval success means the intended physical object is selected without correction, and request time runs from prompt onset to confirmed selection. The physical robot, real components, and complete gearbox sequence support ecological validity; consistent scripts, equivalent trials, and validated multi-item scales support construct validity.

Proposed Evaluation

Success means that the task-aware condition reliably chooses feasible parts, lowers interaction cost without reducing safety or user confidence, and produces reproducible evidence. Each participant’s three conditions will be compared with Friedman tests for ordinal or non-normal composite outcomes, followed—when justified—by paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction. Effect sizes (Kendall’s W and matched-pairs effect sizes), confidence intervals, descriptive statistics, and missing-data counts will accompany p-values. Interaction logs will also be inspected for failure modes such as ambiguous requests, wrong-row selection, incorrect left/right resolution, and recommendations inconsistent with the task graph.
